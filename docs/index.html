    How it works

    1. The microphone is read raw (no noise suppression or auto gain) and resampled to 22,050 Hz.
    2. Every 0.25 s, the last 4 s become a 128 × 173 log-mel spectrogram, computed in JavaScript. It is a port of the Python training code and was checked against librosa: the largest difference is about 1e-7, which is float32 rounding.
    3. A convolutional network (304,289 parameters, converted from Keras to ONNX) runs on it with ONNX Runtime Web and outputs P(gunshot).

    Model v3 learned from 803 gunshots and about 18,800 other sounds (part of them held back for testing) from UrbanSound8K, ESC-50 and FSD50K, including finger snaps, claps, knocks, door slams, typing and fans. On 2,729 test sounds it never saw, it raises false alarms on 2.6% (the previous version: 21.7%). Details, code and error analysis are on GitHub.

    Limitations

    • Research demo, not a safety system. Don't rely on it to detect real gunfire.
    • Fireworks and explosions still fool it (about 1 in 3 test clips), and so can hammering. They really are acoustically close to gunfire.
    • It misses about 40% of gunshots from recordings unlike its training data. Gunshots recorded with other guns, at other distances or on other equipment are harder.
    • A laptop or phone microphone, a speaker or room echo changes the sound. Expect more mistakes than in the published results.
